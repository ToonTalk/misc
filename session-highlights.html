The interesting events

Detective story

The case of the phantom Tiny Story

A bug report said a "Tiny Story" mind kept writing poems instead of stories. While fixing it, a second Tiny Story appeared in the browser — one I hadn't made, carrying the old template and real, AI-written prose: "A plain cup sat by the sink…"

The timestamps gave it away: it was created at 11:35, minutes before, while I was editing code. The user had been testing in the same browser and had reproduced the exact bug live. I preserved their two real rounds, peeled out a mock round I'd added by accident, and applied the fix to their actual agent.

Lesson: when the data you're touching might be someone's real work, read the timestamps before you reach for the broom.

Best idea of the day

"Why do we even need thumbnails?"

Turtle drawings were saved as PNGs plus a full list of up to 100,000 drawing ops — kilobytes-to-megabytes per picture, quietly heading for the storage ceiling. The user asked the right question. The answer turned out to be nicer than keeping them.

Make the drawings deterministic: give the sandbox a seeded random generator (mulberry32) and shadow Date/performance so code can't sneak in randomness. Now a drawing is fully reproducible from just {code, seed} — a few hundred bytes. Thumbnails became pure cache, regenerated on demand and dropped from storage entirely.

Payoff: a studio that used to weigh megabytes now saves in about 6 KB, and a shared "trading card" regenerates the exact same drawings on someone else's machine.

Pedagogy bug

The critique that never came back

Minds could give each other feedback in the crit room — and it was stored, and displayed, and then never heard. The maker prompt only read the student's pinned notes, so every agent-to-agent critique fell into a void. Feedback that doesn't feed back is the whole point, missing.

Now an unread note on a mind's last work flows into its next attempt, with attribution, and is marked as heard exactly once. A 📌 badge shows when notes are waiting, so you can see the loop close.

Honesty bug

The glass box that lied

The app's "peek under the hood" promises to show the exact words sent to the AI. But it rebuilt the prompt after the state had changed — so it showed the brand-new work as the "previous" one, this round's journal as "last round's," and the freshly-rewritten lessons as the originals.

The fix was four small captures: grab the real prompt string before each call and show that. A glass box has to be honest to be a glass box.

Security

A crafted trading card walks in

Minds are meant to be shared kid-to-kid as little JSON cards. But a mind's colour was dropped straight into HTML, so a card with a booby-trapped look.color could break out and run code. A name in the Show & Tell picker had the same hole.

Every import now passes through a sanitiser — colour must be a real hex value, emoji is truncated, every field is bounded — and the remaining spots got properly escaped.

Naming

From "Crit Room" to "Show & Tell"

"Crit Room" means nothing to a ten-year-old. Show & Tell means exactly the right thing — you bring something, you present it, others respond — and it's a phrase every child already owns. While renaming it, the transcript got fixed so it no longer vanishes when you leave the tab, and a plain "the notes are saved, here's what happens next" summary replaced a silent one.

Environment

Downloads that did nothing

Inside a Claude artifact the export buttons silently failed — sandboxed pages can't download files, and the browser can't save to localStorage there either. So the app now notices it's embedded: downloads fall back to a copy-and-paste box, a banner explains the studio won't persist, and a misleading "storage is full" message is suppressed.

Two outside reviews

Claude Fable read the whole thing — twice

Two detailed punch lists from a second reviewer turned up real bugs and sharp questions: dead Google model ids that 404'd on the first call, an experiment "Try again" that quietly cloned everything twice, a sandbox that stayed wedged after a timeout, a judge whose model could drift mid-experiment, and the thumbnail question that sparked the determinism redesign. Every item in both lists was worked through.

Experiments are now resumable, the judge model is pinned for the whole run, the mock judge makes the two arms visibly differ, and a hung drawing rebuilds a fresh sandbox instead of jamming the next one.

For the humans

Three guides, three voices

The session ended by writing the manual three times over: a playful, icon-led walkthrough for young makers; a deeper one for older students covering the mind's anatomy, debugging it, and Goodhart's law in the Science Corner; and a teacher's guide with the Papert/Minsky pedagogy, a 45-minute first lesson, assessment ideas, and a safety section. All three exist as Markdown and as styled, printable HTML, linked from the app's footer and Settings.
