Piskel  +  Pixel Pal

How a free art app learned to give tours

The story of taking an open-source pixel-art editor, folding it into a single file, and moving a friendly AI guide into a panel at its side.

Try it — Piskel with Pixel Pal →

The app we started from

Piskel — free, open source, loved since 2012

Piskel is a free drawing program for pixel art: the chunky, square-by-square style you know from classic video games. You draw on a checkerboard canvas, stack frames like the pages of a flipbook, and out comes a little animation. It has been built in the open for well over a decade by Julien Descottes and a community of volunteers, and its source code is published for anyone to use.

We didn't change a line of it. Everything described below sits alongside the original, politely pressing the same buttons a person would.

Step one: the whole app in a single file

From a folder of hundreds of pieces to one page

A web app normally lives as a folder full of separate pieces: the program itself, the styling, and dozens of little icons and images that get fetched one by one. That's fine on a website, but it can't be handed to someone as a thing.

So the first job was bookbinding. We rebuilt Piskel from its public source code and folded every piece into one self-contained HTML file — every icon converted to text and embedded, repeated artwork stored once instead of thirty-seven times. The result is a single file, a bit under a megabyte, that opens in any browser, works with no internet at all, and can be published as a Claude artifact for anyone to try in one click.

Step two: the guide moves in

Meet Pixel Pal 👾

Down the right-hand side lives a small panel with a guide in it. Press play and Pixel Pal gives a spoken thirteen-stop tour — canvas, tools, colors, frames, layers, all the way to exporting your finished animation — shining a spotlight on each thing as it talks about it. Curious about something? Ask out loud, or type, and it answers in plain words.

The guide can also lend a hand, but only on a short leash. It works the app's real controls — the same buttons you'd press — and it lives by house rules: it never draws on your picture, and anything destructive, like deleting a frame or resizing the canvas, stops and asks you first, in a dialog only you can approve. Click anywhere and it pauses; when it comes back it honestly notices what you did:

"You added a frame and switched to the eraser — carrying on!"

One engine, many guidebooks

Why the second app was so much faster than the first

Under the hood the guide is two things. There's an engine — the voice, the panel, the spotlight, the safety rules — which knows nothing about any particular app. And there's a guidebook written for one app: where everything is, what to say at each stop, which buttons are safe to press. Think of a museum guide's training versus the binder for one specific museum.

The engine was built once, for an earlier app. Piskel needed only its guidebook — and the engine crossed over completely unchanged, which was the whole experiment: proof the split is real.

What the project shelf contributed

Resources that turned a rebuild into a reuse

This project keeps a small shelf of shared resources, and they did a lot of the lifting: the finished engine, ready to drop in; a how-to document distilling everything learned the first time; and a complete worked example — the first app's guidebook and its test suite — to pattern-match against instead of starting from a blank page.

The traffic ran both ways. Porting to a genuinely different app stress-tested that how-to document, and six places where it proved vague or wrong were logged for its next revision. Each new app makes the shelf better for the one after.

How big a job was it?

Building the engine, the first time round, was the mountain. This port took roughly two working sessions of a human and an AI collaborating: the human setting the rules and making the judgment calls, the AI reading Piskel's source, writing the guidebook, and building a test rig that runs the whole app — invisibly, with no screen — and checks 93 things about it.

Those checks earned their keep. They caught real bugs before any person ever saw them: the guide once got pasted into an invisible corner of the app and silently never appeared; a menu closed itself in the very same instant the guide opened it; and the guide was taught a command spelling its own engine didn't recognize. All found by the tests, all fixed the same day.
