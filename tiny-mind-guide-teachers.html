FIELD GUIDE · FOR TEACHERS AND PARENTS

Tiny Mind in the classroom

A browser app where students train a genuinely tiny language model on stories they wrote, then investigate — with real experiments — where the learned knowledge lives.

What it is. Tiny Mind is three linked activities. The Nursery: students invent a character, assemble a storybook, and fine-tune a real 260,000-parameter language model on it, live, in the browser. The Microscope: instruments for looking inside a model while it generates — attention, per-layer predictions, neuron activity, and the ability to switch parts off. The Hunt: a structured investigation — falsifiable clues about where a fact is stored, each proved or busted by experiment. A built-in AI guide narrates throughout (with a younger-reader mode and optional voice), so the app is largely self-teaching; your leverage is in the discussion around it.

WHAT STUDENTS ACTUALLY LEARN

  • What training is: not programming, but repeated exposure driving down a measurable "surprise" — and they watch the number fall.
  • Generalization vs. memorization: the app holds some of their stories out as a secret test set; when held-out surprise stops falling and creeps up, the model has switched from learning the character to parroting the book. Overfitting, experienced rather than defined.
  • Honest evaluation: why you must test on material the learner has never seen — a transferable idea if ever there was one.
  • Forgetting and the replay cure: train only on the new material and old skills decay; students can run the comparison themselves.
  • Correlation vs. causation: the Microscope's motto is "the picture suggested it — the switch-off proved it." Neurons that light up are hypotheses; ablation is the experiment.
  • Distributed representation: the Hunt's designed-in surprise — no single component holds the fact. The in-app metaphor: a fact is like a song in a choir; mute one singer and the song keeps going.
CENTERPIECE ACTIVITY · THE CLASS STORYBOOK

Write the training data together, then train on it

  1. Invent a class character together: a name and three fixed facts. Choose the facts' key words with the app's advice — short, common words make far better hunting targets than rare ones (the app explains why: rare words get spelled from several alphabet pieces).
  2. Everyone writes 2–3 very short stories about the character, keeping all three facts true. The app's storybook checker enforces a young child's vocabulary — a genuine constrained-writing exercise; "simple" turns out to be a discipline, not a dumbing-down. (This also works fully offline from any AI service: hand-written stories are, if anything, the better version of the activity.)
  3. Collect ~40–60 stories into the Nursery. It automatically holds some out as the secret test set.
  4. Train, predicting first. Before pressing start: where will the surprise line flatten? What happens if we keep going long past that? Run it (a few minutes), check predictions, award the parroting badge ceremonially.
  5. Ask it for a story. The class's character, in the model's own words. Read a few aloud; discuss what it got right and what it mangled.
  6. Hunt in groups. Each group takes a fact, gathers clues, and must prove or bust them by switch-off. Prime the choir metaphor before the first bust so failed clues land as discoveries, not disappointments.

Discussion prompts: Why must the test stories stay secret? What did the busted clues tell us that a proved clue couldn't? The name-swap control showed one "fact" survived a name change — was it ever about our character? Each student learned the character from one story; the model needed fifty read hundreds of times — what does each kind of learner have that the other lacks?

PRACTICAL NOTES

  • Use the web version for class — it loads its starter models, the ready-made "Zog mind," and the time-machine arc packs automatically. The Claude-artifact version is convenient for sharing but requires dropping a starter file in when prompted.
  • No accounts; training runs entirely in the browser. Student stories stay on the device. The one optional online feature is the story-writing helper, which sends prompts to an AI service — skip it entirely by writing stories by hand, which the centerpiece activity does anyway.
  • Time: training to the flat point takes a few minutes on an ordinary laptop; the built-in time machine lets you skip the wait by scrubbing a precomputed run, including its deliberately overtrained tail. The ready-made Zog model means the Hunt can start with zero training time if a session is short.
  • Ages: the Nursery works from ~7 with support (little-kid guide mode); the Microscope and Hunt land best at ~10+.
A framing to avoid: "it learns like a child." It doesn't, and the app is honest about this — the one-story-versus-fifty comparison is built in as a discussion moment. The strongest version of this lesson treats the model as a fascinating different kind of learner, not a small person.

BACKGROUND AND PROVENANCE

The models are trained on TinyStories (Eldan & Li, Microsoft Research, 2023; arXiv:2305.07759): ~2 million synthetic stories in a young child's vocabulary, created to show how small a model can be and still produce coherent narrative. The checkpoints are Andrej Karpathy's, from his open-source llama2.c project. These are architecturally the same species as Claude or ChatGPT — next-token predictors — but by credible estimates tens of thousands of times smaller (the 15M specimen) to millions of times smaller (the 260K trainee). That reduction is the whole pedagogical trick: frontier models are far too large to open; a tiny one fits under a classroom microscope. Dataset license CDLA-Sharing-1.0; code and checkpoints MIT — attribution is in the app's About.
