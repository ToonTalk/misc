Guide in a box · a note for curious non-programmers

Every app can have a guide now.

A small robot docent that sits beside an app, gives a spoken tour, answers questions out loud, and can point at things — or even press a button for you, with your permission. Building one used to be a project. Now it's a briefing. This page explains what changed. It also has a guide of its own, so it can show you rather than tell you.

Stop 1 · What a guide is

A docent, not a pop-up

You've met the bad version: a tutorial that covers half the screen, makes you click "Next" nine times, and vanishes forever. This is the other thing. The guide lives in its own slim panel at the edge of the app — the app politely makes room for it, so it never covers your work. It talks you through a tour, stop by stop, shining a gold spotlight on each thing it mentions. You can interrupt it at any moment with a question, spoken or typed, and everything it says out loud is also written down in its panel, like a transcript, in case you'd rather read.

And it has hands, used carefully. Ask it to open something for you and it will — but only after asking "shall I?", and only by pressing the app's own real buttons, never by magic behind the scenes.

Stop 2 · The hard part

Good manners are the real work

Here's the surprise: the tour itself — deciding what to say at each stop — is the easy bit. Almost all the effort goes into the guide's manners: the dozens of small behaviours that make it feel like a courteous colleague instead of a pushy salesman. They were learned one mistake at a time, across several apps.

House rules, learned the hard way

Never cover the work.

The app reshuffles itself to make room for the panel — at every window size, even on a phone. A visitor, especially a child, won't know to close an overlay.

The moment you touch anything, it stops talking.

Click anywhere in the app mid-tour and the guide pauses itself. You're in charge; it carries on only when you press play.

It notices what you did while it was paused.

"Ooh — I see you've accepted the boss's assignment. Let's carry on from there." It never takes credit for your moves, and never nags you about steps you've already done.

It proposes; you approve.

Anything that changes your work needs your yes, through a proper in-page dialog. Pointing and showing stay instant.

It copes with awkward rooms.

Some places the app runs — like a preview inside a chat window — quietly ban microphones and pop-ups. The guide detects this and explains honestly what will and won't work there, instead of failing in silence.

Rebuilding all of that from scratch for each new app meant re-making — and sometimes re-discovering — the same mistakes every time.

Stop 3 · What changed

Train the docent once; write new tour notes per museum

A good museum guide isn't retrained from zero for every exhibition. The training — how to speak, when to yield, how to ask permission, what to do when the microphone is banned — is done once. For a new museum, the guide only needs a briefing pack: the route, the exhibits, what they're allowed to touch.

That's exactly the split we just made. All the manners now live in one shared, well-tested engine. Giving a new app a guide no longer means rebuilding the guide — it means writing its briefing.

The trained guide

Written once · shared by every app

The voice, the panel, the spotlight, the pausing-when-you-click, the what-did-you-change memory, the may-I-please dialogs, the awkward-room diplomacy. All of it, packaged.

The briefing pack

Written per app · the only new work

The tour stops and what to say at each. What the guide may point at. The short list of things it's allowed to do for you. A page of introductions to this particular app.

The first app has already been re-fitted this way, and behaves exactly as it did before — down to the wording — which is how we know the split was clean.

Stop 4 · The recipe

A playbook an AI can follow

These apps are built in conversation with an AI assistant, and the guide engine comes with a written playbook for exactly that: here is the trained guide; here is what a briefing pack contains; here are the house rules and why each one exists. Fitting a guide to the next app becomes an afternoon's conversation instead of a week's construction — describe the tour, review what the assistant proposes, try it.

The playbook also insists the assistant show its work: what it checked, what it couldn't check, and any judgment calls it made along the way.

Stop 5 · Trust, but verify

125 little inspections, every time

How do we know the guide still behaves after every change? An automated inspection puts it through its paces in a simulated browser: start the tour, pause it, poke the app, resume, ask questions, refuse a permission, grant one, try the awkward rooms. One hundred and twenty-five separate checks, run twice for good measure, all of which must pass before anything ships. Politeness, enforced by checklist.

The tour never presses a story button: the big moves in an app belong to the person using it. The guide points, and says whose move it is.

For the curious: the trained guide is one file (vg-core.js), a briefing pack is another (about half its size), and a build step folds both into the app so the result is still a single file you can email to a friend. The playbook is voice-guide-prompt-v3; the inspection is smoke-guide.
