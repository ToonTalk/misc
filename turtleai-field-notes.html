Field notes · TurtleAI benchmark · visual program synthesis

Drawing turtle graphics from a picture, one careful look at a time

TurtleAI (Wen, Staub & Singla, ACL Findings 2026) gives a model a target image and asks for the Python turtle program that reproduces it. This is a record of 26 attempts across three rounds of increasingly disciplined methodology — blind single-shot, then a cleaner blind single-shot, then iterative refinement against the benchmark's own pixel-comparison oracle — with every render kept, including the wrong ones.

Round 01

Round One

Six tasks, blind, one shot each

A random draw of six tasks. For each: look at the target image once, write a draw(t) program from that single look, submit it, and only then look at how it rendered. No self-checking before scoring — with one disclosed exception (task 3 below, where I broke discipline and iterated against my own offline simulator before committing).

Round 02

Round Two

Fifteen tasks, stratified 5 / 5 / 5 across DSReal, DSCraft and DSSyn

Same single-shot rule as round one, applied cleanly throughout this time: every task got exactly one look, one program, one submission, before any rendering. Tasks were drawn to cover all three dataset sources evenly, so the result reads less like an anecdote and more like a genuine (if still tiny) sample.

Round 03

Round Three

Five tasks, iterative, oracle-checked

A different rule: render, compare against the target, diagnose, fix, and repeat — capped at a handful of rounds per task — using the benchmark's own pixel-comparison function as a pass/fail oracle without reading the reference code itself. Where a task still resisted after several honest attempts, I looked at the reference afterward, purely to explain the remaining gap rather than to keep pretending it was still unsolved.

2/5 solvedDSReal 1/1  ·  DSSyn 1/4
specimen

syn_0536_midi_8g

DSSyn composite medium 5 attempts
NOT SOLVED
→ → → → →

A wide rectangle, a large diamond (a square rotated 45°), and three shrinking squares staircasing toward the corner where everything meets. Attempt 1 was a rough gestalt guess. Measuring the target's pixel bounding box directly (row/column density scans) nailed the rectangle at 300×150 and the diamond's true centre and radius almost exactly — attempts 2–3 converged fast once I stopped eyeballing.

The part that never closed: the three small shapes alternate upright → diamond → upright (each level adds another 45° of rotation to the previous one, and a square's 90° symmetry makes that alternate between looking 'upright' and 'diamond'), which I did eventually notice and model in attempt 4. What I couldn't pin down in the time I gave it was the exact repositioning rule between levels — how far to step, and in which heading, before drawing the next smaller shape. Five attempts in, still short.

midi_8e

DSReal translation easy 2 attempts
CORRECT
→ →

Six vertical ticks on a baseline. Attempt 1 was, to my eye, pixel-identical to the target — and it still failed. That gap is the whole point of this task: I measured the real image's tick columns by column-density (not by looking), and found the baseline actually runs about 100 units past the last tick with no seventh mark there. A structural fact invisible from casual side-by-side comparison of two similar-looking thumbnails. Attempt 2, corrected, matched exactly.

DSSyn rotation medium 3 attempts
NOT SOLVED

A dense radial burst of spokes with visibly uneven lengths. A radial pixel-histogram of the target (binning distance-from-centre by angle) turned up a suspiciously clean 40° repeat — but that measurement was almost certainly picking up line-width smearing across angular bins near the centre, not 63 genuine spokes, and I couldn't fully untangle signal from artefact in the time I gave it.

specimen

syn_0249_midi_1b

DSSyn rotation medium 6 attempts
CORRECT
→ → → → → →

Six five-pointed stars arranged in a ring. By attempt 4 I had measured the ring radius (148), the angular spacing (an exact 60°, offset 56.1° from due east), the star size, and analytically derived the offset between a star's drawing-start-vertex and its own centroid — and the resulting placement matched the target's measurements to within 1% on every number I could check. It still failed.

Attempt 5 swept five blends between 'every star drawn upright' and 'every star rotated to match its position on the ring' — on the theory that partial ring-alignment might explain small per-star bounding-box differences I'd measured. All five failed too.

What finally closed it wasn't more precision — it was recognising the wrong generator. Both my code and the reference move the turtle with forward/right, which is polar motion either way — distance and heading — so that was never the real difference. The actual gap: I gave my program a persistent shared origin, one centre computed once and then addressed all six times over by radius and angle. The reference has no origin at all — every step is relative to wherever the turtle already is, a chain of moves where each state depends only on the one just before it, nothing held in common across stars. Draw a star, step forward a fixed distance, turn 60°, repeat six times. Six 60° turns sum to 360°, so a regular hexagon falls out of that chain for free — the 'ring' was a side effect of dead reckoning, never a design. This is the same 'draw shape → step forward → turn' idiom that showed up, differently parameterised, in three other tasks in this report. Recognising the family beat re-measuring the geometry.

syn_0185_midi_11j

DSSyn composite medium 2 attempts
NOT SOLVED
→ →

A large jagged near-circle built from a repeated small motif, plus a tiny plain circle marking the start point. Attempt 1 got the gestalt — scalloped ring, small circle in the right place — without getting the tooth shape right. A zoomed crop of the target's teeth showed each unit is actually two crossing line segments (a near-flat one and a steeper one that overshoots and crosses back over it), not a simple symmetric zigzag.

Attempt 2, aimed at that crossing-barb shape, overshot into something much spikier and lost the smooth ring entirely — a worse match despite being a more 'informed' guess. Reverted to attempt 1 as the better of the two and stopped there.

What actually explains the misses

  • Undercounting repeated elements. At least four separate tasks failed because I counted one fewer repetition than the reference — a staircase, a colour alternation, a fan of squares — always in the same direction. That's a specific, checkable prior about my own failure mode, not just "spatial reasoning is hard."
  • Visual similarity isn't sufficient evidence. midi_8e in round 3 is the clearest case: attempt 1 looked pixel-identical to the target and still failed the benchmark's own comparison. Measuring the target directly (column-density scans, connected-component centroids, radial histograms) caught structural facts — an unmarked trailing segment, a five-fold instead of six-fold symmetry — that side-by-side eyeballing never would have.
  • Measurement finds the shape, not the generator. The six-stars task in round 3 is the sharper lesson: a fully measured, sub-1%-error placement against one shared, fixed origin still failed — not because it wasn't "polar enough" (turtle motion is polar either way), but because the reference program has no persistent origin at all, just a chain of relative forward/turn moves each depending only on the step before it, with a regular hexagon falling out for free once six 60° turns sum to 360°. The fix wasn't more precision, it was recognising a code idiom I'd already reverse-engineered in three other tasks that round.
  • The synthetic half of the dataset (DSSyn) is a different, harder problem than the real-world half. Across all three rounds, DSSyn tasks scored lowest, and twice the reference solution itself turned out to be geometrically sloppier than my own reasoning (a non-staggered "hex tiling," a checkerboard with a fill rule tied to an orientation flag rather than a clean parity check) — ground truth generated by an earlier LLM pass, imperfections included.
