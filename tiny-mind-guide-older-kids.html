Tiny Mind is three connected apps. In the Nursery you train a real language model on stories about a character you invent. In the Microscope you inspect a model’s insides while it thinks. In the Hunt you run actual experiments to find where a learned fact lives. An AI guide sits in the corner of each — it introduces itself as “a mind too, a much bigger one” — and will walk you through everything below, including a simpler mode for younger kids.

WHAT A LANGUAGE MODEL IS (60 SECONDS)

A language model does one thing: given some text, it guesses the next little piece. That’s all. Stories, answers, everything — just very good next-piece guessing. The trainee’s entire alphabet is 512 pieces, and the app shows all of them on one screen, lit up by how often your storybook uses each one. Watch for your character’s name: it isn’t one piece — it gets spelled from several, which matters later in the Hunt.

RAISING ONE

Invent a character with three fixed facts. Build a storybook (~50 short stories — write them or co-write with the app’s helper; a checker keeps the vocabulary simple, which is harder than it sounds). Then train, and read the chart like a scientist:

The surprise meter — “how surprised is it by Zog stories it has never seen?”
Falling = learning. When it flattens (often around a hundred steps), the model knows your character as well as it ever will. Those test stories are kept secret from training — that’s the only honest way to measure learning.
Parroting — “what if I keep training anyway?”
Past the flat point, the secret-story surprise creeps back up: the model is memorizing your exact storybook instead of learning your character. Scientists call it overfitting. You get a badge when it happens.
Forgetting — “did it lose its old skills?”
Train only on Zog and its ordinary storytelling decays. The replay mix (old stories stirred in) prevents it — try 0% and watch what happens.

The time machine lets you scrub through a training run like a video — including one that runs deliberately past the flat point into parroting — and “keep raising it from here” makes any moment the start of your own run.

THE INSTRUMENTS

Guess-o-meter — “what does it think comes next?”
The model’s actual probabilities for the next piece, live.
Attention view — “where is it looking?”
When the model picks a word, which earlier words is it reading? Watch it glance back at the name when it has to continue a sentence about your character.
Logit lens — “watch a guess grow.”
The model has layers; this shows its best guess at each layer — a thought sharpening as it rises.
Neuron finder — “which cells light up?”
Finds neurons that fire on your character’s name. Careful: lighting up is a clue, not proof.
The switch-off test — “prove it.”
Actually disable a part and measure what breaks. The app’s own motto: the picture suggested it — the switch-off proved it.

THE HUNT, AND ITS TWIST

The Hunt turns those instruments into a game: get falsifiable clues about where a fact is stored, then prove or bust each one. Expect busts — that’s the discovery. A fact in the model is like a song in a choir: mute one singer and the song keeps going; mute the whole front row and it collapses. Knowledge is spread out, not filed in one cell. There’s also a sneaky control experiment: swap the character’s name and re-ask. If the answer doesn’t change, the “fact” was never really about your character at all — it was just a common word pattern. Real scientists run exactly this kind of check.

Honesty box. You learned Zog from one story; the model needed fifty, read hundreds of times, and it still stores him as a smear across many parts. It does not learn the way you do. That difference is one of the most interesting things the app can show you.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

The models here were trained on TinyStories (Eldan & Li, 2023): about two million tiny stories, machine-written in the vocabulary of a young child, made to test how small a model can be and still tell a coherent tale. The checkpoints come from Andrej Karpathy’s llama2.c project. And they are the same species as Claude or ChatGPT — the same next-piece-guessing design. Nobody outside the labs knows exactly how big those are, but reasonable estimates make the 15M specimen tens of thousands of times smaller and your 260K trainee millions of times smaller. Too big to open; that’s why we raise tiny ones.

ADD THE BIG SPECIMEN (OPTIONAL)

stories15M.bin is a 15-million-parameter storyteller — the file is the model: about 58 MB that is nothing but the learned numbers. Download it and its alphabet file, then drop both on the app’s models tab:

model: https://huggingface.co/karpathy/tinyllamas/resolve/main/stories15M.bin
alphabet: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/karpathy/llama2.c/master/tokenizer.bin

It tells much better stories than the trainee — put it under the Microscope and compare their insides.

TWO WAYS TO RUN IT

The web version loads its starter models and arc packs automatically — start there. The Claude-artifact version is handy for sharing; it can’t fetch files itself, so you drop the starter pack in when it asks (the guide walks you through it).
