Pre-WWI Parallels with Today

The structural rhyme between 1914 and now is loud, even if the lyrics differ.

Hegemonic transition is the deepest parallel. Rising Germany challenged British dominance; rising China challenges American primacy. Graham Allison's Thucydides Trap data suggests roughly three-quarters of such transitions have ended in war — a sobering baseline, not a prophecy.

Alliance rigidity compounds the danger. The Triple Alliance and Triple Entente turned a Balkan assassination into a continental catastrophe. NATO, AUKUS, and the emerging Sino-Russian entente are hardening similar blocs, shrinking diplomatic maneuvering room. Worse, the feedback loop is self-reinforcing: defensive alliance-building reads as aggression to rivals, triggering counter-alliances that validate the original fear.

Economic interdependence offers false comfort. Pre-1914 Europe was deeply integrated — Norman Angell famously declared war therefore impossible. It wasn't. The "too interconnected to fight" logic about US-China relations deserves the same skepticism.

Nationalism and revisionism create flashpoints — Ukraine, Taiwan, the South China Sea — where prestige can override rational calculation, just as it did in the Balkans.

The most important disanalogy is nuclear deterrence, which genuinely has no 1914 equivalent and meaningfully constrains escalation. The most important lesson isn't that war is inevitable but that 1914's leaders sleepwalked into it — driven partly by domestic political pressures and elite miscalculation rather than pure systemic forces. Today's equivalent risks include populist-driven foreign policy, social-media-inflamed publics, and the near-total absence of functioning great-power diplomatic architecture to manage crises before they cascade.
