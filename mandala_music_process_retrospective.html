Executive summary

The early versions treated the animation as a generic stream of visual features: brightness, density, complexity, saturation, and motion magnitude. That produced reasonable ambient music, but not the kind of perceived synchronization you were looking for. The major conceptual shift was to treat the video as a mandala motion problem: rotations, direction reversals, outward/inward pulses, and center-driven expansion/contraction are perceptually meaningful and need their own musical mappings.

The final direction was the polar-gated approach: rotation-dominant sections suppress pulse/bass gestures and emphasize swirl/panning/arpeggios; pulse-dominant sections suppress swirl gestures and emphasize center-pulse bass, breathing envelopes, and rising/falling responses.

Timeline of the process

1. Proof of concept: sampled visual features → algorithmic WAV

What changed: Started with low-resolution frame samples and generated a plausible ambient soundtrack using brightness, density, saturation, complexity, and motion magnitude.

What we learned: Good for mood, weak for perceived sync.

2. WebM muxing and frame-source repair

What changed: Initial WebM mux/re-encode attempts produced short or slow-motion outputs. We moved to rebuilding from the authoritative 9000 PNG frames at 30 fps.

What we learned: The PNG sequence was the reliable source of truth; stream-copying WebM timestamps was not.

3. Full-PNG analysis and VP8 WebM builder

What changed: Built a clean Colab pipeline: 19 ZIPs → 9000 extracted PNGs → full-sequence feature analysis → WAV → VP8 WebM.

What we learned: The technical video/audio pipeline became stable.

4. Sync diagnostics

What changed: Compared visual motion curves with audio loudness and onset curves, using 30-second and later 10-second clips.

What we learned: The first versions were more ambient than synchronized; simple global audio shifts were insufficient.

5. Tight-sync event layer

What changed: Added event detection: motion peaks, complexity peaks, density/brightness changes, and color shifts. Hard events triggered bells, bass pulses, and glassy accents.

What we learned: Clearer onsets, but still too many mid-level events blurred the sync.

6. Hierarchical sync

What changed: Separated hard events from soft events so only strong motion/complexity peaks got obvious accents.

What we learned: Less cluttered, but still missing the perceived difference between rotation and center-pulsing.

7. Polar-motion analysis

What changed: Added optical-flow-derived radial and tangential features: rotation dominance, pulse dominance, outward/inward flow, and rotation direction proxy.

What we learned: This matched the visual perception better: early ≈ rotational/mixed, middle ≈ pulse-dominant, late ≈ mixed.

8. Polar-gated music mapping

What changed: Gated layers by visual regime: rotation sections emphasize swirl/panning/arpeggios; pulse sections emphasize bass/breathing/rise-fall gestures.

What we learned: This produced the most defensible mapping and the version we decided was likely good enough.

What the final mapping tries to express

  • Motion magnitude affects musical energy and event density.
  • Pulse dominance drives bass, breathing, outward rising gestures, and inward falling/collapsing gestures.
  • Rotation dominance drives circular panning, swirl arpeggios, and direction-sensitive glassy accents.
  • Rotation and pulse reversals become discrete musical pivot events.
  • Hard events are sparse, obvious accents; soft events mainly color the texture.

Why we probably stopped at the right point

By the end, the project had crossed the useful threshold from “ambient accompaniment” to “defensible algorithmic score.” Further improvements would mostly be subjective composition and mixing choices: more or less percussion, stronger or weaker swirl, brighter or darker timbre, fewer or more accents. Those are worth changing only after watching the full five-minute render and identifying a concrete annoyance.
